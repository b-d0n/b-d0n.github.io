PROJECT / 01

Electric Displacement Catamaran

Princeton Electric Speedboating

Led the conversion of a sailing Nacra 17 catamaran hull to electric propulsion using a 48V outboard and 1000A battery system. The conversion included a custom hydraulic steering system, flooring and decking integration, a planing surface, and a custom plywood/carbon-fiber outboard mounting plate. The completed vessel achieved speeds of approximately 15–20 mph during competition.

01

Decking Structure

The decking structure was constructed using 40 mm aluminum extrusion beams and connected to the pre-existing hull crossbeams through custom 3D-printed mounts. Aluminum extrusion was selected for its strength, modularity, and lower cost relative to stainless steel alternatives. To verify that the structure could support the driver, battery, and steering system, FEA was performed in Fusion 360.

To coordinate the project across a multi-person team, a shared CAD assembly of the catamaran was created to guide design integration, fabrication, and assembly while improving communication and keeping the team aligned on the overall system architecture.

PROJECT / 02

3D Printed Star Tracker

Personal Project

When photographing galaxies, nebulae, and stars, astrophotography requires the camera to move at the same apparent rate as the night sky. Commercial tracking mounts can maintain approximately ±0.05°/s of tracking precision, but often cost $400–$1,000. I aimed to build my own for a fraction of that cost using stepper motors, 3D-printed components, and an Arduino Uno R3.

V1

Version One

The first design used a worm-gear drive with the stepper motor directly driving the worm. This version was relatively bulky, but it successfully captured 30-second exposures at a 135 mm focal length. Using an Arduino and custom code, the mount could also automatically move between different galaxies and nebulae in the sky. Tracking performance was ultimately limited by inconsistent meshing between the 3D-printed worm gears.

V2

Version Two

The second design prioritized compact size for easier travel and carry-on storage while also addressing the gear-meshing issues from V1. V2 used belt-driven GT2 pulleys with two 1:3 reduction stages for a total 1:9 gear reduction, producing smoother tracking and increasing torque from the stepper motor for a 3.5 lb payload. This version achieved 45–60 second exposures at a total build cost of approximately $35.

PROJECT / 03

DIY Reef Tank Chiller

Personal Project

The goal of this project was to apply my knowledge of thermodynamics to a problem I faced during the summer: keeping my five-gallon reef aquarium at a steady 78°F on hot days to prevent heat stress and coral loss. Traditional hobby-grade chillers often cost more than $200 and are sized for much larger systems, so I set out to build a compact system capable of lowering the tank temperature by at least 8°F and maintaining 78°F despite higher ambient room temperatures.

01

Thermal Design

The system was built around a TEC1-12706 60 W Peltier module, with a large heat sink and fan on the hot side and aluminum square stock on the cold side. Before building the prototype, I used first-principles heat-transfer analysis and equations from my thermodynamics coursework to estimate the system heat load and confirm that the cooling assembly could achieve the desired steady-state temperature of 78°F.

02

Design & Prototype

Using a salvaged 12 V computer power supply and a digital thermostat breakout board, I wired the system to switch on automatically and maintain 78°F. The Peltier cold side was coupled to painted aluminum stock to maximize heat transfer while isolating the aluminum from the saltwater. Titanium would have been the preferred material for long-term reef compatibility and corrosion resistance, but it was cost-prohibitive. The final system successfully maintained the reef at a constant temperature for a total build cost of approximately $30.

Design, Testing, and Optimization of a 30 kW Electric D-Stock Hydroplane

Testing Lead · Princeton University

Worked as part of a four-person team to design, build, and test an electric D-Stock hydroplane aimed at breaking the APBA 72-volt kilometer speed record of 49.997 mph. The platform combined a ZeroJet 30 kW motor-inverter, a custom 54 V battery pack, a custom geared outboard, and a dual-loop cooling system. I led testing, using controlled load-disk and on-water experiments to evaluate powertrain performance, thermal behavior, propeller slip, and drivetrain setup.

46 mph Final top speed
19.8% Final propeller slip
72% Load-disk efficiency
01

Testing & Validation

Led staged load-disk testing to validate the outboard structure, sustained power capability, battery current, and thermal performance. Tests reached 60 Nm of motor torque and approximately 420 A of battery current while monitoring RPM, torque, voltage, current, and temperature data.

02

On-Water Optimization

Iterated propeller pitch, trim angle, torque limits, and final drive ratio across three water-test configurations. Top speed increased from 26 to 46 mph while propeller slip fell from 52.5% to 19.8%, bringing the platform within 4 mph of the standing record and identifying gearing and propeller selection as the highest-impact next steps.
